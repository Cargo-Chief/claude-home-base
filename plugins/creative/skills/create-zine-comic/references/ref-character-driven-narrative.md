# Page 2: The Problem It Solved

A landscape-format educational comic book page (2048x1440px) with a clean white/light cream background and a structured 4-column × 2-row grid layout, giving us 8 slots to work with. Panels can be combined for emphasis — this is a comic book, and the artist has freedom to merge slots for impact.

The style is polished, modern comic book — clean lines, vibrant colors, professional typography mixed with handwritten-style annotations. Sophisticated and well-designed. Comic book meets beautiful iPad notes.

IMPORTANT: Do NOT number the panels. No circled numbers or digits in the top-left corners of panels. The layout should flow naturally without explicit numbering.

## The Star: Claudie

The recurring character is "Claudie" — a small, cute, blocky orange pixel-art creature with four short legs, two square black eyes with small eyelashes, and often holding a small clipboard. See the attached reference image for the exact character design. Claudie is friendly, competent, and slightly amused. Appears across panels as the guide/narrator.

## John Gruber — Consistent Character Design

John Gruber appears in MULTIPLE panels on this page and MUST look like the same person every time. He is a man in his mid-30s, brown hair, glasses with dark rectangular frames, wearing a simple dark t-shirt. Clean-shaven. Slightly nerdy, slightly cool — think indie tech blogger, not corporate programmer. He looks like a guy who writes well AND codes. Draw him in the same consistent comic-book style across every panel he appears in — same face, same hair, same glasses, same proportions. When he reappears, the reader should instantly recognize him.

## Goal of This Page

Tell the origin story. The setup: in 2004, there were no website builders like Squarespace, no blogging platforms with rich text editors. If you wanted to put your writing on the internet, you had to wrap every word in HTML — a markup language that web browsers read. HTML still exists today (every website still runs on it), but it's incredibly verbose. Want to bold a word? That's `<strong>word</strong>`. Want a bullet list? That's `<ul><li>item</li></ul>`. Gruber was a programmer AND a writer — he could write HTML, but he hated that something so simple required so much ceremony. His fix was embarrassingly elegant. The reader doesn't need to know HTML — the page should make the verbosity self-evident.

## Panel Layout

### Slot 1 (top-left, 1 slot) — Title Panel
Bold comic-style title "The Problem It Solved" in large dark charcoal lettering with an orange accent underline. Below in smaller text: "A programmer who just wanted to write." Claudie stands with arms crossed, looking slightly disgusted — like someone who's about to show you something unnecessarily complicated. A small speech bubble: "Brace yourself."

### Slots 2-3 (top-center, merged 2 slots) — "The Year Is 2004"
A scene-setting panel that tells a mini-story in three beats, reading left to right. The SAME character (John Gruber — mid-30s, brown hair, dark rectangular glasses, dark t-shirt) appears in all three beats and must look identical each time:

**Beat 1 — "He had something to say":** Gruber sitting at his desk with an early-2000s Apple laptop (white iBook era), looking enthusiastic. He's just finished writing a blog post — you can see clean text on his screen. A thought bubble: "Great post. Let me put this online." Coffee cup on the desk. The vibe is creative energy — a writer who's excited about what he wrote.

**Beat 2 — "But the internet spoke a different language":** The same Gruber, same desk, same laptop — but now his expression has shifted to frustrated resignation. His screen now shows the same blog post but wrapped in HTML tags — angle brackets everywhere, `<p>`, `<strong>`, `<a href>`, `<ul>`, `<li>`. The clean writing is buried in code. A caption below this beat explains: "In 2004, there was no Squarespace. No WordPress editor. If you wanted to publish on the internet, you had to write your words inside HTML — the code that web browsers read. It still exists today — every website runs on it."

**Beat 3 — "Look at this mess":** A zoomed-in view of the HTML on his screen, large enough to read:

```
<p>Here are my <strong>top three</strong>
picks:</p>
<ul>
  <li>The Great Gatsby</li>
  <li>Dune</li>
</ul>
```

Handwritten annotations point out the absurdity:
- Arrow to `<strong>...</strong>`: "All this just to bold two words"
- Arrow to `<ul><li>...</li></ul>`: "All this for a bullet point"
- Big annotation at bottom: "His actual words are maybe 30% of this. The rest is instructions for the browser."

### Slot 4 (top-right, 1 slot) — "Who Is This Guy?"
A character card for Gruber. The SAME character from the previous panel — mid-30s, brown hair, dark rectangular glasses, dark t-shirt — now shown in a portrait-style composition, arms crossed, looking at the reader with a "yeah, I'm going to fix this" expression.

Text at top: "John Gruber"
Below: "Programmer. Writer. Ran a popular tech blog called Daring Fireball."

A key line in handwritten style: "He COULD write HTML. He just thought it was absurd that something so simple required so much ceremony."

Claudie sits at the bottom of the panel, looking up at Gruber approvingly.

### Slots 5-6 (bottom-left, merged 2 slots) — "His Fix Was Embarrassingly Simple"
The big reveal. A dramatic side-by-side with a bold "VS" comic-burst divider:

**Left half — "What you had to write (HTML):"**
The HTML version of a short blog post. Dense, cluttered, angle brackets everywhere. Rendered with desaturated, cold color treatment — stressful to look at:

```
<h1>My Favorite Books</h1>
<p>Here are my <strong>top three</strong>
picks:</p>
<ul>
  <li><em>Gatsby</em> — still holds up</li>
  <li><em>Dune</em> — finally read it</li>
  <li>Freakonomics — <strong>everyone</strong>
  is talking about this</li>
</ul>
```

**Right half — "His idea:"**
The exact same content in markdown. Clean, readable, warm colors:

```
# My Favorite Books

Here are my **top three** picks:

- *Gatsby* — still holds up
- *Dune* — finally read it
- Freakonomics — **everyone**
  is talking about this
```

Below both halves, a highlighted callout: "His insight: what if the plain text already LOOKED like what it meant? Stars around a word obviously means emphasis. A # at the start obviously means a heading. You can read it without knowing any code at all."

Annotation pointing to the markdown side: "Same content. Anyone can read this one."

### Slot 7 (bottom-center-right, 1 slot) — "A Weekend Project"
Gruber again — the SAME character, same glasses, same t-shirt — now shown from behind, sitting at his desk late at night. The screen shows a small code file, clearly short (fitting on one screen). The filename visible: "Markdown.pl". A coffee cup (now empty) beside him. The lighting is warm and late-night.

Bold text: "He built it in a few weekends."

Below: "A tiny program: you write the simple version, it converts to the HTML browsers need. He called it 'Markdown' — because the whole point was LESS markup, not more."

Claudie at the bottom, looking at the small script with raised eyebrows: "That's... it?"

### Slot 8 (bottom-right, 1 slot) — "He Had No Idea"
Same Gruber silhouette at his desk, but now the screen shows clean markdown text instead of HTML. Warm glow. He looks satisfied — he solved his problem.

Bold text: "He published it. Barely updated it. Moved on."

Below: "It's almost identical today to what it was in 2004. It didn't need to change."

Hook for next page, larger text: "He had no idea what was about to happen."

Claudie at the bottom, pulling the panel corner like turning a page: "It gets wild from here."

## Visual Design Notes

- Thin light gray grid lines separating panels, with 8px rounded corners on each panel
- CRITICAL: John Gruber must look like the SAME person in every panel — same face, hair, glasses, clothing, proportions. He appears in slots 2-3, slot 4, slot 7, and slot 8. Character consistency is essential.
- Color palette: HTML panels use cooler, desaturated tones. Markdown panels use warmer, cleaner tones. The late-night coding panel (slot 7) has warm lamp-light tones.
- The VS panel (slots 5-6) is the emotional centerpiece — the contrast should be striking at a glance.
- Gruber should feel relatable — a creative person frustrated by unnecessary friction, not a stereotypical programmer.
- Typography mixes clean sans-serif for main text with casual handwritten font for annotations
- NO panel numbers anywhere — the layout flows naturally
- Same visual style and feel as the rest of the Claude Code Zines series
